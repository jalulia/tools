A mathematical theory of communication

Claude Shannon’s 1948 paper did three things that had not been done before. It gave information a unit. It showed that any source of messages has a measurable rate, and any channel a measurable capacity. And it proved that noise, however bad, leaves a limit you can reach but never pass. Fig. 1 in the paper · Introduction

Four boxes, one wire, and a noise source

Fig. 1 · a general communication systemrunning
Source → transmitter → signal → receiver → destination. The one box nobody had drawn before is the noise source. Everything in the paper follows from taking it seriously.

Shannon’s first move is to draw the whole problem as one diagram. A source picks a message. A transmitter turns it into a signal. The signal crosses a channel, where noise is added. A receiver turns it back into a message for the destination.

The second move is to say what the problem is not about. Meaning is irrelevant to the engineering. The system has to work for every message the source could pick, so what matters is the set of possible messages and how likely each one is, never what any one of them says.

Raise the noise. The letters are sent as 5-bit teletype code, the way the paper counts them. Each flipped bit lands somewhere in a letter.

§6 Choice, uncertainty and entropy · Fig. 7

Information is surprise, and the unit is the bit

Fig. 7 · entropy of a two-way choiceH = 1.000 bit
A fair coin carries one bit per flip. A coin that lands heads 9 times in 10 carries 0.469 bits, because you can mostly guess it.

Shannon asks: given a set of possible messages with known probabilities, how much choice is there? How uncertain is the outcome? He wants one number, and he lists what it must do. It should be largest when every outcome is equally likely. It should grow when there are more outcomes. And when a choice is made in two stages, the number for the whole should be the weighted sum of the numbers for the stages.

Only one formula satisfies all three. He calls it entropy, after the identical formula in physics.

H = −Σ pi log2 pi   bits per symbol

Two equally likely outcomes give 1 bit. Eight give 3 bits, because 2³ = 8. The log is what makes the unit add up: two independent choices carry the sum of their bits, not the product of their counts.

A source is a set of habits

Shannon built these by opening a book at random and copying what followed. Here they are built from a short corpus of plain English, written for this page.

A source that picked letters at random would be a terrible model of English. Real English has habits: E is common, Q is followed by U, THE is everywhere. Shannon models a source as a process with memory, where each symbol depends on the ones before it.

Then he shows what the habits buy. Each approximation adds one more layer of structure, and each one looks more like English than the last. The famous observation is that the fake text stays plausible for about twice the span its rule accounts for: pairs of letters give readable triples, pairs of words give readable phrases.

The entropy falls with every layer. Structure is predictability, and predictability is fewer bits. That is the whole reason a source has a rate at all.

The numbers are the entropy of each model, measured on the corpus. Shannon’s own estimates for printed English, counting structure out to about eight letters, come to roughly 2.3 bits per letter, and lower still at long range.

§7 The entropy of an information source · redundancy

English is about half redundant

Deleting letters0 % removed
Redundancy = 1 − (actual entropy ÷ the maximum for the alphabet). For English, Shannon puts it near 50 %.

If a letter of English carries about 2.3 bits but could carry 4.75, half of every letter is doing no work. Shannon calls that fraction redundancy, and he gives it a plain meaning: it is the share of the text you could delete and still recover.

He also points out that it cuts both ways. Redundancy is waste on a clean line, so a good code squeezes it out. On a noisy line it is protection, because the receiver can use it to repair damage. The rest of the paper is about doing both on purpose.

His aside about crosswords is a test of the number: at 50 % redundancy, large two-dimensional crosswords are just possible. At 33 %, three-dimensional ones would be.

§9 The fundamental theorem for a noiseless channel · the example in §10

Short codes for common things, and a floor you cannot go under

A four-symbol source and its best codeH = 1.750 bits

Best code, average1.75bits / symbol
Fixed 2-bit code2.00bits / symbol
Drag the bars to change the probabilities. The code is rebuilt each time; the average length can equal H when the probabilities are powers of ½, and never go below it.

Morse code already knew the trick: E is one dot. Shannon proves the general rule. A source with entropy H bits per symbol can be encoded into about H binary digits per symbol, as close as you like, and no code can do better. Common symbols get short codes, rare ones get long codes, and the average lands on H.

This is the paper’s first fundamental theorem. Capacity and entropy are in the same unit, so dividing one by the other tells you how fast a given source can go through a given channel.

symbols per second → C / H, and never more

The example is Shannon’s own, §10: four symbols with probabilities ½, ¼, ⅛, ⅛. The best code is 0, 10, 110, 111, and it averages exactly 1¾ bits.

§12 Equivocation and channel capacity · §13 the theorem · §17 an example of efficient coding

Noise sets a limit, and the limit can be reached

A binary channel that flips bitsC = 1.000
Equivocation0.286bits lost
Capacity0.714bits / bit sent
Send 3×, vote0.7% wrong, rate ⅓
Capacity is what is left after the receiver’s doubt is subtracted. At a 50 % flip rate nothing gets through; at 0 % every bit does.

Noise does not just add errors. It adds doubt: after the receiver sees a symbol, there is still some uncertainty about what was sent. Shannon measures that leftover uncertainty in bits and calls it equivocation. The rate of a noisy channel is what you sent minus what remains in doubt. Capacity is the best rate any source can reach.

C = max [ H(x) − Hy(x) ]

The obvious defense is repetition: send every bit three times and take a vote. Errors drop, but the rate drops to a third, and to drive errors toward zero that way you would drive the rate toward zero too.

The theorem says otherwise. For any rate below C, there exist codes with error as small as you like. The limit is a line, and you can stand right next to it. Shannon’s proof picks codes at random and shows that almost all of them work, which is why the paper could prove the limit exists without being able to build a code that reaches it.

§17 · one error in seven, correctedclean
d1d2d3d4c1c2c3
Four data bits, three check bits. Tap any bit to flip it in transit. The receiver recomputes the checks and the pattern of failures points at the damaged bit.

The paper ends Part II with one constructive example. If a channel damages at most one bit in every block of seven, four of the seven can carry data and three can carry checks, for a rate of 4⁄7. Each check watches a different subset of the data bits. Which checks fail tells the receiver which bit to flip back.

This is the code Hamming published two years later. Shannon describes it as an example and gives the rate; the full theory of such codes was still to come.

§25 Channel capacity with an average power limitation · Theorem 17

Bandwidth times the log of signal over noise

A continuous channel29.9 kbit/s
Capacity29.9kbit/s
Bits per second per Hz9.97
Samples that matter6000per second
A telephone line, 3 kHz at 30 dB, comes to about 30 kbit/s. Doubling the bandwidth doubles capacity; doubling the signal power adds one bit per second per hertz.

Real channels carry waveforms, not symbols. Shannon gets from one to the other with the sampling theorem: a signal limited to bandwidth W is fully described by 2W numbers per second. A waveform of duration T is a point in a space of 2TW dimensions.

Then the argument becomes geometry. Signals with power P sit in a sphere. Noise with power N smears each point into a small sphere around it. The number of signals you can tell apart is the number of small spheres that fit inside the big one, and taking the log of that number per second gives the capacity.

C = W log2 (P + N) / N   bits per second

Every modem rating since is a reading of this formula. It also says what the best signal looks like: to reach the limit, the transmitted waveform should be statistically like white noise.

The drawn waveform is a band-limited signal with noise added at the chosen ratio; the dots are the 2W samples per second that carry it.

What the paper leaves out, on purpose

Meaning

Shannon’s measure counts choices, not content. A page of nonsense and a page of poetry with the same letter statistics carry the same number of bits. This is the price of the theory and the reason it works: because it ignores meaning, it applies to every message, every language, every channel.

Three things to carry out of the paper:

  • Information is measured by how much it removes uncertainty, in bits, and the measure is the log of the number of equally likely possibilities.
  • A source has a rate and a channel has a capacity, in the same unit, so the question “how fast can this go through that?” has a numerical answer.
  • Noise sets a limit on the rate, and the limit is reachable with vanishing error. Above it, nothing helps; below it, there is always a code.

Part V, which this page does not draw, asks the next question: if the receiver will accept some distortion, how many bits does the source really need? That is the beginning of lossy compression, and of every audio and image format you use.
