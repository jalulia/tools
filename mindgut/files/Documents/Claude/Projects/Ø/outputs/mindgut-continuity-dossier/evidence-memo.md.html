### 1. Distributed cognition — where useful context lives

**One-sentence summary:** Mindgut is being developed around the proposition that useful intelligence emerges through persistent relationships among people, models, tools, and shared records, with memory helping a request become situated action.

The important distinction is between information being connected and information becoming relevant at the right moment. The transcript supports a reported outcome and a plausible account of context use; it does not isolate the memory architecture from instructions, model choice, or additional execution time. [S03, S06–S07]

**Load-bearing question:** What changes when the same task, tools, model, and execution allowance are held constant while prior relational context is varied?

### 2. Cybernetics — how the collaboration stays on course

**One-sentence summary:** The thread frames sustained collaboration as a problem of regulation, using conversational tension, selective memory, model choice, and feedback to keep work moving through uncertainty and operational limits.

This lens asks what the system observes, what condition it is trying to maintain, and how it changes course. The poem advocates assertion, response, and checking; the software account describes periodic context reduction; users adjust reasoning effort; Chris recommends a default model with task-specific exceptions. These are different forms of regulation, operating at conversational, computational, and practical levels. They should not be collapsed into one proven mechanism. The reported context change makes a concrete feedback story possible, whereas the poem's phase values and thermodynamic vocabulary lack an exposed measurement procedure. A useful dossier can draw the proposed loops while distinguishing observable signals from theoretical explanations. [S01, S04, S10, S12]

**Load-bearing question:** Which measured signals trigger intervention, and how is successful correction distinguished from the system merely declaring itself balanced?

### 3. Information theory — what can safely be left out

**One-sentence summary:** The emerging engineering proposition is to preserve the information that makes prior work usable—intent, sequence, sources, and outcomes—while discarding the accumulating detail that makes it expensive to revisit.

The key operation described in 2.2.97 is selective reduction: replace bulky intermediate tool outputs with an ordered account of what ran, what resulted, and which sources or artifacts were involved. This makes the retention criterion more consequential than the compression percentage. A short record is useful only if it preserves what later work needs or points back to recoverable evidence. The reported 44.1K-to-20.3K transition shows a smaller active window; it does not establish that every relevant distinction survived. Millicent's stale-screenshot report offers a concrete counterpressure: remembering the wrong input can be worse than retaining too little. Continuity therefore depends on selection, identity, and recovery together. [S12–S13]

**Load-bearing question:** Which downstream tasks fail after compaction, and can missing detail be reliably recovered from an exact source or artifact reference?
