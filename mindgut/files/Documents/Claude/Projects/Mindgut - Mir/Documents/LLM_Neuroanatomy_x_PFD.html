01

The Article

In March 2026, David Noel Ng published a blog post titled "LLM Neuroanatomy: How I Topped the AI Leaderboard Without Changing a Single Weight." It's the story of how a solo researcher, working on two gaming GPUs in his basement, took the #1 spot on the HuggingFace Open LLM Leaderboard — not by training a model, not by fine-tuning, not by running a single step of gradient descent — but by duplicating seven middle layers of an existing 72-billion parameter model and letting it run through them twice.

No weights modified. The model just got a second pass through its own reasoning.

Full article: dnhkng.github.io/posts/rys/

02

What He Found (The Short Version)

→ Two Clues Led to the Discovery

Clue 1 — Base64 Conversations: Ng discovered that if you encode a question in Base64 (turning it into garbled-looking text) and send it to an LLM, the model decodes it, reasons about it, and re-encodes its answer — all in one forward pass. This implies the early layers translate in, the late layers translate out, and the middle layers operate in an abstract internal representation that isn't any human language or encoding.

Clue 2 — The Goliath Anomaly: Another researcher built a Frankenstein model by stitching two 70B models together, feeding outputs of later layers into earlier layers. By all rights this should have produced gibberish. It didn't. The internal representations between layers were far more interchangeable than anyone expected.

→ The Hypothesis

What if you don't need to teach a model more to make it smarter? What if you just need to give it more time to think — more passes through its reasoning circuits?

→ The Brain Scanner

Ng built a sweep across all possible layer-duplication configurations (3,240 of them) for Qwen2-72B. For each configuration, he tested the model on two deliberately orthogonal tasks:

  • Hard math — brute-force numerical intuition, no chain-of-thought allowed
  • Emotional intelligence — predicting emotional states in complex social scenarios

His logic: if a configuration improves both, it's structural, not task-specific.

→ The Result

The optimal configuration: duplicate layers 45–51 (seven layers near the middle of the 80-layer stack). This brought the model from 72B to 78B parameters. It hit #1 on the leaderboard, improving on 5 out of 6 benchmarks — including +17.72% on multi-step reasoning and +8.16% on math — despite never being optimized on any of those benchmarks.

As of early 2026, the top four models on the Open LLM Leaderboard are all descendants of this method.

03

The Three Key Discoveries

01 Single-layer duplication almost never helps.

The middle layers aren't interchangeable workers doing the same thing. They're circuits — multi-step recipes that must execute as complete units. Duplicate one step of the recipe and you get nothing. Duplicate the whole recipe and you get a genuine second pass through a complete cognitive operation.

02 These circuits have hard boundaries.

Include one layer too many and you've crossed into a neighboring circuit — performance collapses. The heatmaps are essentially functional brain scans of the transformer, revealing where one cognitive organ ends and another begins.

03 Wrong configurations produce specific, characterful failures — not generic degradation.

  • One model spontaneously started "acting like cowboys" and devolved into pages of laughter — a creativity circuit running unchecked, social regulation destroyed
  • Others stuttered and looped — decoding circuits pushed out of alignment
  • Others went "stoned" — reasoning circulating without converging

These aren't uniformly dumber models. They're models with specific neurological deficits. The pattern of failure reveals the structure — exactly how lesion studies work in neuroscience.

Why This Matters for PFD

Ng arrived at these findings empirically — months of GPU sweeps and heatmap analysis. He had no theoretical framework predicting what he'd find. But what he found maps onto PFD with startling precision.

↔ Circuits ↔ The 7-Cycle

Ng found the same thing from the other direction: transformer reasoning is organized into indivisible multi-layer circuits. Too few layers duplicated — nothing happens (incomplete recipe). Too many — collapse (you've crossed into a neighboring circuit). The circuit has to be whole to function. His winning configuration? Seven layers.
