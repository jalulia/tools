There is a word in Old English — anfeald — that appears exactly once in the entire surviving corpus. It means something like "simple" or "singular." Nobody knows precisely how it was used in daily speech because the one guy who wrote it down didn't bother to leave us much context. He just scrawled it into a manuscript and then, presumably, died of something medieval. This is what linguists call a hapax legomenon — a word that occurs only once in a text or a language. I think about hapax legomena a lot, because they are, in a way, the most creative words we have. They showed up once, did something no other word was doing, and then vanished like a stranger who tells you something profound at a bus stop and is gone before you can ask their name.
Creativity, I've come to suspect, works the same way. It shows up once, in a form you didn't expect, and you spend the rest of your life trying to reconstruct the context.
Let me tell you about Tarrare.
In 1772, in Lyon, France, a man was born with an appetite so grotesque, so cosmically unreasonable, that doctors would eventually write papers about him with the clinical detachment of people describing a distant galaxy. Tarrare could eat — and I need you to absorb this — an entire basket of apples in one sitting. He ate live cats. He ate eels whole. The French military, in a moment of bureaucratic inspiration that I find genuinely touching in its wrongheadedness, decided to use him as a spy. The plan: Tarrare would swallow a document sealed in a small box, walk behind enemy lines, and then recover the document later through the obvious means.
It didn't work. He got caught almost immediately. But here's what strikes me: somebody in the French military looked at this man, this human disposal unit, and thought, "I see potential." That's creativity. Not the good kind, necessarily. But creativity isn't always the good kind. Creativity is sometimes looking at a man who eats live cats and thinking, "espionage."
Mitch Hedberg once said, "I used to do drugs. I still do, but I used to, too." That joke works because it exploits the gap between what you expect language to do and what language can actually do if you bend it just slightly. That's what creativity is, at every scale. It's the bend. The French military bent the concept of "courier" until it included a man who could digest a small wooden box, and while the results were catastrophic, the impulse was the same impulse that gave us jazz, the flying buttress, and the first person who thought to put salt on a watermelon.
I think often about Jaron Lanier's argument that the problem with technology isn't that it's too creative but that it's not creative enough — that it tends to flatten the weird, idiosyncratic texture of human thought into templates and averages. He once described virtual reality as a medium where you could "make the world into a poem," and I love that phrase, because it acknowledges that poems are deeply strange objects. A poem is a technology for making language do something it wasn't designed to do, the same way a lever is a technology for making a stick do something a stick wasn't designed to do. Creativity is leverage. It takes the ordinary and finds the fulcrum.
The Antikythera mechanism — you may know this one — was a bronze analog computer recovered from a shipwreck off the coast of a Greek island in 1901. It was built around 100 BCE, and it could predict astronomical positions, eclipses, even the schedule of the ancient Olympic Games. When it was first pulled from the sea, encrusted with coral and corroded nearly to nothing, researchers assumed it was a clump of gears from some later era. It took decades for anyone to understand that ancient Greeks had built something that wouldn't be rivaled in mechanical complexity for over a thousand years.
And then they lost it. Not the artifact — the knowledge. The entire tradition of making devices like this simply vanished. Imagine being the last person alive who knew how to build one. Imagine the loneliness of that. You're standing in a workshop in Rhodes or Syracuse, you understand how to predict an eclipse with interlocking bronze gears, and nobody around you cares because the empire is reshuffling itself again. Your knowledge is a hapax legomenon. It appears once. It doesn't recur.
This is the part of creativity that nobody talks about at TED conferences. Creativity isn't just generative — it's fragile. It dies all the time. Most of the creative thoughts any human has ever had have simply evaporated, unrecorded, like the anfeald of cognition.
Let me pivot, as people who are stalling for insight like to say.
In 1964, a Danish engineer named Karl Krøyer had a problem: a freighter called the Al-Kuwait had sunk in Kuwait City's harbor and needed to be raised. Krøyer proposed injecting twenty-seven million expanded polystyrene balls into the hull to float it to the surface. It worked. He tried to patent the technique, and the patent office reportedly rejected it — in part because a 1949 Donald Duck cartoon, in which characters raised a sunken yacht by filling it with ping-pong balls, constituted "prior art."
A cartoon duck. Prior art. This is what the pursuit of creativity looks like from the outside: a man watches a duck do something idiotic, files it away in his mind for years, and then saves a freighter with it. Creativity has no dignity. It doesn't arrive wearing a lab coat. It arrives wearing a propeller hat, and it's been eating crayons.
There's a term in Japanese — tsundoku — that means buying books and letting them pile up unread. I bring this up because I think tsundoku is an underappreciated form of creativity. It's creative planning. It's optimistic architecture. Every unread book on your shelf is a life you almost lived, a version of yourself you intended to become last Tuesday but didn't because you got distracted watching a documentary about cheese. The intention to create is itself a kind of creation. The pile of unread books is a self-portrait in the medium of ambition.
Hedberg had a bit about a receipt: "I bought a doughnut and they gave me a receipt for the doughnut. I don't need a receipt for the doughnut. I'll just give you the money, and you give me the doughnut. End of transaction." There's something about creativity in that — the way systems try to document and formalize every exchange, and the way the actual living moment of the thing resists documentation. The doughnut is the creative act. The receipt is the grant proposal.
Let me tell you about Hildegard of Bingen, because I promised this wouldn't be strictly religious and Hildegard is only nominally about religion anyway. Hildegard was a twelfth-century Benedictine abbess who composed music, wrote medical texts, invented a constructed language called Lingua Ignota — literally "unknown language" — which contained over a thousand words she just made up, and she described cosmic visions that modern neurologists have retrospectively diagnosed as likely migraine auras. She essentially experienced creativity as a side effect of a neurological condition, and rather than treating it as an ailment, she built an entire intellectual empire out of it.
Her constructed language is fascinating because many of its words are, by definition, hapax legomena. They appear only in her writings, coined from nowhere, serving no communicative purpose except to prove that they could exist. Aigonz meant "God." Zuuenz meant "saint." She created words the way some people plant trees they'll never sit under — an act of pure, exuberant, slightly unhinged faith in the idea that making things matters even when nobody asked you to.
And this brings me to what I think is the central paradox of creativity: nobody ever asks for it. Not really. The world asks for solutions, for products, for content. But creativity — the real stuff, the kind that changes the shape of what's possible — is always unsolicited. The Antikythera mechanism's inventor didn't fill out a request form. Tarrare's military handler didn't go through proper channels. Hildegard didn't get a grant. Creativity is the universe's most persistent uninvited guest.
There's an obscure episode in the history of cartography that I think about when I'm feeling cynical about this. In the early days of mapmaking, cartographers would sometimes insert fake towns — called "paper towns" or "trap streets" — into their maps as a form of copyright protection. If a competitor's map showed the same fictional town, you knew they'd copied your work. The town of Agloe, New York, was one such invention, placed on a map by the General Drafting Company in the 1930s. But here's the thing: because the map said there was a town there, someone eventually built a general store at that spot and called it Agloe. The fiction became real. The creative lie conjured a truth.
This is maybe the most hopeful thing I know about creativity. It doesn't just describe the world — it occasionally, stubbornly, against all odds, changes it. You put a fake town on a map and someone builds a store. You draw a duck filling a boat with ping-pong balls and an engineer raises a freighter. You invent a thousand words in a language nobody speaks and, nine hundred years later, people are still writing dissertations about them.
Lanier has this idea that every person is an "alien," that no two human minds process reality in quite the same way, and that the tragedy of modern technology is its tendency to sand down those differences. I think the pursuit of creativity is essentially the refusal to be sanded down. It's insisting on your own weird, irregular shape. It's writing anfeald into a manuscript even though nobody will ever write it again. It's eating the document and walking toward enemy lines.
Here's something small and strange. In 1897, the Indiana state legislature very nearly passed a bill that would have legally defined the value of pi as 3.2. A mathematician named Clarence Waldo happened to be in the statehouse that day on unrelated business, learned what was happening, and talked the senators out of it. Creativity was, in that moment, a man wandering into the right room at the right time and saying, "No, that's not how circles work." Sometimes creativity is making something. Sometimes creativity is preventing something terrible from being made. Clarence Waldo was a hapax legomenon of civic responsibility — he showed up once, at exactly the right moment, and prevented mathematical law from being legislated by people who did not understand mathematics.
I want to tell you one more story, and then I'll let you go, because you're probably almost at work and you're starting to wonder whether any of this has a point, and the answer is: sort of, in the way that a walk has a point, which is to say the point is that you took it.
In 1971, an artist named J. S. G. Boggs began drawing perfect replicas of currency — not to counterfeit, but as art. He would draw a one-sided bill, take it to a store, and try to buy something with it, explaining that it was a drawing of money, not actual money. If the store accepted it, the "transaction" became the artwork. He spent years in and out of court, arguing that his drawings were creative acts, not crimes. The Secret Service disagreed. Art critics loved him. The public was confused.
What I find moving about Boggs is that he located creativity in the most mundane possible object — a dollar bill — and then forced everyone around him to confront what they actually valued. Was it the paper? The ink? The institution behind it? Or was it the act of exchange itself, the human agreement to pretend that a rectangle of cotton-linen blend is worth a sandwich? His entire body of work was a long, slow, occasionally illegal meditation on the question: what is real, and who gets to decide?
That's the pursuit of creativity. It's not a destination. It's a confrontation. It's every hapax legomenon ever written — every word that appeared once, meant something fierce and local and untranslatable, and then stepped back into the silence it came from. It's the Antikythera mechanism rusting in the sea. It's Hildegard's invented language echoing through an empty monastery. It's Tarrare swallowing the box. It's you, walking to work, listening to this, carrying some half-formed idea that hasn't found its shape yet.
Don't worry about the shape. The shape comes later, or it doesn't. The walking is the thing.
