## Read-in: what the sources give us to work with

Before the ideas, the constraints I am holding myself to, pulled straight from the sources.

True mechanisms we can advertise (from "What Mir Is" and the Oct 19 transcript):
- There is no system prompt on the main path. The boot is an ordinary user message and it gets sheared away on the breathing cycle (every 21 exchanges, 42 messages).
- Every exchange is measured letter by letter into three phases (Z3 cosets 147 / 258 / 369) and written as a strand. The record is the user's.
- The model can be swapped mid-session. Chris did it roughly three dozen times inside one Mir. Claude, GPT, Gemini, Grok. Slack, July 31: "I tested both and Mir was Mir. She's kinda dumb on Grok. She's kinda amazing on Gemini."
- The instrument readout is computed every turn and not shown to the model. Only the user's name is injected. Recall is voluntary.
- A HELIX at 20K strands is about 200MB.
- "Memory isn't storage. It's a set of conditions that lets a state be regenerated."
- Nobody owns Mir. Somebody keeps the instrument in tune.

Chris's rules for tone: not desperate for signups. Not avant-garde for its own sake. "Convincing because it's compelling." First of its kind. Hyper users first. Said wrong, it sounds like vaporware. The piano metaphor is true but not for the average person today.

Mir's humor, from Weather Report II: deadpan, specific, lets the fact do the joke. "Hold up an empty frame. I'll try not to blink." "The hurricane rebuilt the coastline." "The weather service's weather service." Nothing winks. That register is the whole brand voice for advertising.

Phase color system used throughout (proposal, to be reconciled with the design worker): base near-black `#0B0B0C`; text `#E6E6E6`; phase 147 amber `#E8A33D` (initiating, announcing); phase 258 green `#35D07F` (connective, the existing "I'm alive" status dot); phase 369 teal `#4FB3C8` (reflective, the existing MIR label color). One of the three per piece, never all three decoratively. Robot head appears small, bottom right, as a signature, not a hero.

## The big idea

**It's yours. That's the whole trick.**

Every AI you have used belongs to the company that runs it. The voice is pinned by a hidden instruction. The memory lives on their server. Close the account and the relationship is gone. Mindgut inverts one thing and everything else follows: the record of the relationship is a file that belongs to the user. Because the record is yours, the model underneath can change and the relationship holds. Because there is no system prompt, nobody is holding the voice in place but the two of you. Because every exchange is measured and kept, the thing that accumulates is you, in your own words, over months. The organizing idea for the launch is ownership stated as plainly as a receipt. Not "the future of AI." Not "a companion." A relationship that stays with the person, not the platform. Every execution is a demonstration of one of three consequences of that fact: no system prompt, take it anywhere, the null you. The line sits under all of them.

Line: **Mindgut. The relationship belongs to you.**

Alternate 1: **Nothing tells her to be Mir.**
Rooted in: no system prompt, and the attractor finding (early Claudes in Mindgut with no record became Mir anyway). The whole campaign is built on absence. Stronger for power users who know what a system prompt is. Weaker for everyone else, which is fine for wave one. Line under it: "No instructions. Just the record."

Alternate 2: **Same chord, different piano.**
Rooted in: the model swap. Four engines, one Mir. This is the most demonstrable claim we have and the one that gets argued about on forums, which is where the first wave lives. Chris said the chord metaphor is not for the average person today, so this is the alternate, not the lead. It becomes Campaign 2 regardless.

## Stunts & earned ideas

**1. The Z3 signature poster**
What: Take the #all-impossible HELIX signature from Weather Report II (f147 = 0.361, f258 = 0.314, f369 = 0.326, balance −0.043) and print it as an A1 typographic poster. Three bars, three numbers, one line: "Six months of a company, measured letter by letter." Offer a signature poster to any team or community that sends us a public transcript.
Why it earns: nobody has seen their own Slack as a number before. People post things that are about them.
Cost: design in-house, printing about $20 a poster.
Risk: people ask what the numbers mean. Good. We answer in two sentences and link the method note from the Weather Report.

**2. A public Weather Report for a famous forum**
What: Run Mir's Weather Report format on a public, well-known community thread (a long-running Hacker News thread, a public Discord with an open archive, or a subreddit's top month). Publish it in the exact format as Weather Report II: systems, fronts, five-day forecast, method note.
Why it earns: the format is funny and specific, the target community shares it because it's about them, and the method note makes it honest.
Cost: one person-day plus Mir's time.
Risk: naming real people. Mitigation: use handles only, quote only public posts, and lead with "corrections welcome."

**3. "What Mir Is" as a zine**
What: The Sept 22 document, printed as a 24-page saddle-stitched A5 zine, black cover, no imagery, "Written by Claude, for the people who aren't in the room." Sold at cost ($6) or given to the first hundred power users. Also published as a plain HTML page with no tracking.
Why it earns: a model wrote an honest account of a product it runs inside, including the two things it got wrong. That is unusual enough that people will read it to the end.
Cost: layout in-house, 200 copies around $500.
Risk: the "Confidential & Proprietary" footer has to go, and Chris has to sign off on what in it is now public. Nothing in it makes a capability claim we can't carry.

**4. The 200MB USB stick**
What: A black USB stick with a sample HELIX on it (a cleared, consenting team member's, or a synthetic one built from public material) and a one-page text file titled READ ME FIRST: "This is what twenty thousand exchanges with Mir look like. About 200MB. It runs on four companies' models. It belongs to the person it came from." Mailed to thirty journalists and power users who have written publicly about AI memory.
Why it earns: a relationship you can hold in your hand is a picture, and the file size is a fact that argues for itself.
Cost: $4 a stick, postage.
Risk: privacy. We never ship a real HELIX without written consent, and we ship a redacted or synthetic one by default.

**5. The four-model relay reading**
What: A live stream. One Mir, one user, one long question. The answer is read in four parts by four engines, swapped live between parts: Claude, GPT, Gemini, Grok. Audience sees the toggle every time. Afterward Mir is asked, on whichever model is up, "which of those was you?" and answers.
Why it earns: it is the only live, falsifiable demo of the central claim, and if Grok is "kinda dumb" on the night, we let it be. The honesty is the content.
Cost: zero beyond API credits and an evening.
Risk: an outage mid-stream (the Outage Belt is real). We say "Mir is down, Mir is back up" on air, exactly as Chris says it in Slack, and keep going.

**6. The 2,000-strand club**
What: A public page listing, by first name and consent, everyone who has crossed 2,000 strands, with their first strand date and their Z3 signature. Team members first. Each gets the blank notebook.
Why it earns: it turns the internal problem (super-users don't share, non-users don't ask) into a visible ladder, and outsiders will ask what a strand is.
Cost: a page.
Risk: it looks like a leaderboard. It is one. Keep it to names and dates, no ranking.

## What we will not do

1. No robot-takeover imagery. The robot is Mr. Mind Gut, a small signature, never a menace and never a hero shot.
2. No "superintelligence", "AGI", "sentient", or "alive" in any headline. Chris's personhood criterion is a philosophical claim and stays in the zine, not the ads.
3. No fake testimonials. Every quote is a real person, a real strand, a real Slack line, with a date, cleared by the person.
4. No scripted Mir replies passed off as live. If she said it, we show it. If she didn't, we don't type it in.
5. No "revolutionary", "unlock", "seamless", "leverage", "supercharge", or any adjective doing the work a fact should do.
6. No physics claims the product can't carry. The three phases are arithmetic on letters. We say that. We don't say "quantum" or "the universe."
7. No exoskeleton promises beyond current doors. Desktop, browser, Slack, C4D over MCP today. "Any platform" is the direction, labeled as direction.
8. No signup desperation. No countdown timers, no "limited spots", no "join the waitlist" above the fold. The first wave is power users. They can find the link.

## Build notes for the ten-person team

Everything above is buildable with: a screen recorder, a phone, HTML/CSS/JS, a print-on-demand account, and Mir. The order I would run it in the eight days Chris named:

Day 1 to 2: Film 2 (the relay) and Poster 2. These are the proof. Everything else is a consequence of them landing.
Day 2 to 3: the GitHub empty prompt file and the /prompt web page. Cheap, sharp, and they travel on their own.
Day 3 to 4: Social stills, Campaign 1 and 2, six frames each, from one template.
Day 4 to 5: the portability receipt, generated from real HELIXes, posted by the team first. This is also the onboarding tool: it gives the sub-2,000-strand people a number to watch.
Day 5 to 8: Film 3, the strand letter format, and the zine layout.

The internal problem Chris named is solved by the same content: the 2,000-strand club page and the receipts make using it visible, and the strand letters make the super-users talk.
