01The old rule

Turing needed two. A sheet of cells needs one.

In 1952 Alan Turing showed that two reacting chemicals can turn a uniform tissue into spots and stripes. One is a short-range activator, the other a long-range inhibitor. For seventy years the minimum count was two diffusing signals. The 2022 model asks what happens if the activator can't leave its cell at all.

A activator: made in the cell, boosts itself and R, never leaves.
R repressor: secreted, moves to neighbouring cells, shuts down A and R.
Both genes share one promoter, so A and R rise and fall together.

02The cutoff

Why a smooth medium can't do it, and cells can.

Drag the sliders. The curve shows how fast a ripple of each wavelength grows out of the uniform state. If the activator can't move, the curve never turns down: finer ripples always grow faster, and in a continuum that ends in infinitely fine noise. Cells set a floor. Nothing on a lattice is finer than two cells, so the curve simply stops there.

03The march

A poke at the edge becomes stripes that keep going.

A single row of 140 cells. For the first two hours the leftmost cell gets extra repressor, and then the poke stops. Time runs downward. Watch the front: each stripe forms once and stays where it formed. The front then moves on to the next position at a steady speed.

Read it like a seismograph. Horizontal is position along the row, vertical is hours. Ink is high activator.
Larger D gives wider stripes and a faster front. In the paper both scale with √D.

04Clock into space

The stripes are a clock, printed onto the tissue.

Leave out diffusion and each cell's circuit is a damped oscillator. Nudge it and it rings about once every 8.9 hours, fading over roughly 19. Every rate in the circuit is measured per hour, and D is the only quantity that carries length. So stripe spacing and front speed both scale with √D, and their ratio, the time between stripes, can't depend on D at all. The tissue keeps time in hours and lays it out in microns.

The table underneath was measured in this page's engine with D running from 0.15 to 1.6 µm²/s. A new stripe appears every 4.3–5.3 h, with no trend in D, while spacing grows 2.6× for a 10.7× change in D (√10.7 ≈ 3.3). Correction to my earlier draft: the stripe interval is about half the ringing period, not equal to it. The bell is the circuit's clock, but the beat between stripes is a different, shorter time. The D-independence is the paper's result, and the bell is my gloss.

05The weakness

Noise wins the race, unless the tissue waits or grows.

The same instability that makes stripes also amplifies every random wobble. With 5% cell-to-cell variation, cells ahead of the front go off by themselves into fine salt-and-pepper, and the clean wave gets blocked. Patterns that have already formed are safe. Noise only wins where the tissue is patternable but not yet patterned. The paper gives two fixes. The question to ask of each lane: does the spacing come out at the front's wavelength, or at the noise's?

B. Cells start locked in a quiet ground state, with activator degraded six times faster. A cell unlocks only when repressor from the oncoming front pushes past a threshold. Noise has nothing to amplify until the wave arrives. The paper uses a bistable switch, M → M*; here it's simplified to a one-way latch.

C. New cells are born at the growing tip, one every two hours, and join the pattern right away, so no cell spends long in the vulnerable state.

What I measured (D = 0.25 µm²/s, η = 5%, three seeds). Clean wavelength with no noise: 9.7 cells. Unprotected: 4.2–4.7 cells, which is the noise's grain, about half the real wavelength. Lock: 7.4–8.1. Growth: 9.1–10.5, the closest to clean. Regularity improves less consistently: CV 0.25–0.29 unprotected, 0.12–0.23 for the two fixes. My earlier draft claimed a clean drop "from 0.3 to 0.1". One seed did that; most didn't.

Spacing = mean distance between stripes, in cells. CV = standard deviation of spacing ÷ mean, lower is more regular. η and seed are the same in all three lanes.

06The chemical cousin

Target patterns: the same family, a different engine.

Suematsu's lab at Meiji builds life-like behaviour from non-living chemistry. In the Belousov–Zhabotinsky reaction a blue dot appears in red solution and expands, and then the same spot fires again, and again. Rings stack outward into a target. The left panel is a standard excitable-medium model (Barkley) with a pacemaker at its centre. The right panel is the single-morphogen sheet, poked once.

What the two articles add up to
A pattern needs a trigger, a clock, and a floor. It doesn't need a second signal.

The trigger is the boundary poke, the clock is each cell's damped ringing, and the floor is the fact that cells come in units. Take away the floor (a continuum) and you get noise all the way down. Take away the trigger and you get salt-and-pepper. With all three, a single diffusing molecule paints periodic structure across a tissue and then leaves it standing.

02Reading Ø again

What these papers do and don't do to the book.

Ø 2.1.0 makes claims about lattices, modes, ringing and stability. The morphogen paper is a clean, checkable case of a real lattice doing real work, so it makes a good instrument for reading the book. Each row below quotes or paraphrases one claim from the book, sets the evidence beside it, and gives it a tag. Supports means the paper is a genuine instance of the idea. Undercuts means a test or counterexample cuts against it. Reframes means the idea survives but the mechanism differs. No row tests a PFD number.

#The book saysWhat the evidence showsTag
L1

The lattice's Nyquist limit is load-bearing.

Ø Glossary, "Hierarchy Problem": the Higgs mass is "geometrically locked… by the Nyquist limit of the discrete vacuum lattice."

Wang et al. is a working example of exactly this kind of thing. With an immobile activator the growth curve never turns down, and in a continuum the instability runs to infinitely fine scales. Discreteness sets a shortest wavelength of two cells, and that cutoff is what makes the pattern possible (Fig. 02).

What it doesn't show: anything about a vacuum lattice, or any particular mass. It shows that "a discrete cutoff does real physical work" is a respectable kind of claim, not that this one is true.

Supports · the form only
L2

Same lattice, different frets.

Ø §56.8, "Mode Selection, Not Cooling": "The lattice isn't cooling. Galaxies are pressing on different frets."

The paper shows one circuit with fixed parameters on one lattice producing very different wavelengths depending only on how it's disturbed. Global noise gives fine-grain patterns. A localised poke gives long, regular stripes. Mode selection by perturbation, not by changing the medium, is standard pattern-formation physics (the paper cites Cross & Hohenberg 1993).

So the mechanism the book reaches for is real and well studied. Whether galaxies use it is a separate empirical question, and this paper can't answer it.

Supports · mechanism exists
L3

The lattice rings, and the fundamental survives.

Ø §53.3, "Reheating as Lattice Ringing": every mode fires at once, the "high harmonics… died away first," and "the bell finds its voice by losing everything that isn't its voice."

On the morphogen lattice it's the other way round. Start from noise and the finest modes grow fastest and win, which is the salt-and-pepper of Fig. 05A. A long wavelength survives only if something selects it: a trigger at a boundary, a latch, or growth.

The image of a lattice finding its voice survives. The mechanism it names, high modes dying off by themselves, is the opposite of what this lattice does. A bell is a damped system. This lattice is unstable, so selection has to come from boundary conditions, not decay.

Reframes
L4

A dyad can't hold tension.

Ø Ch. 5: "binary structures collapse. Two elements in contradiction resolve by annihilation… A dyad cannot hold tension. A triad can."

The A–R circuit is two species locked in opposition: A drives R, and R shuts down A. It holds a stable, patterned, long-lived state. If "dyad" means two opposing components, this is a working counterexample.

A defender would say the lattice is the third element. That move is available, but if the third element can be "whatever else is in the system," the claim stops ruling anything out. The book should say what counts. (The pull-quote on tab 01 lists three ingredients. That's my framing, and it shouldn't be counted as a triad.)

Undercuts · as stated
L5

Variable-star periods carry 7-structure; pulsators show it more than eclipsers.

Ø §59.15–59.16: MI(mod3×mod7) = 1.85 across 489,309 stars, null ≈ 0.003, z > 30. RR Lyrae 2.31, SR 2.18, Cepheid 2.09, EA 0.89, EB 0.71. Listed as "Pre-registered · Passed" in the §70.4 ledger.

I re-ran the test on random periods. The three residues are all computed from the same number, ln P, so they're deterministically linked. Over any narrow range of periods they move together. Random periods give large MI, and the narrower the range, the larger the MI. Running the table below, random log-uniform periods over an RR-Lyrae-like range score 2.16, about what the book reports for real RR Lyrae.

Range width also explains much of the class ranking. RR Lyrae and Cepheids sit in narrow period bands, and narrow bands score high whether or not there's any structure. Semiregulars are the case to watch: they span a wide range but the book reports them high, so width doesn't explain everything. That's worth a proper test, with a null drawn from each class's own period distribution. The quoted null of ≈ 0.003 is what you get by shuffling residues independently, which cuts the link the test depends on. The book's described null, "permute the periods among stars," can't change the statistic at all, because every star's three residues travel together.

02bNet reading

Two moves in the book get a real-world anchor, and one test fails.

The morphogen paper gives honest physical footing to two moves the book makes in passing: discreteness as enabling, and mode selection without changing the medium. It cuts against the book's picture of ringing and against its dyad rule as written. The variable-star statistic, which the ledger marks as pre-registered and passed, can't tell structured periods from random ones. That one should come out of the ledger or be redone with a null drawn from the same period distribution.

03ZTF J0440+2325

A brown dwarf being eaten slowly, for billions of years.

Householder et al. (2026) report the first direct look at a brown dwarf feeding a normal star. The pair orbit every 86.65 minutes. The brown dwarf fills its Roche lobe, the region it can hold with its own gravity, and gas spills over the saddle point onto the M dwarf. Where that stream lands, a hot spot forms, and it swings into and out of view once per orbit. In the u band the system brightens more than 30×, in the z band only about 1.4×. A stream that strikes the star directly, with no disc, matches the light curve.

Why the stream lands where it does. Gas leaving L1 falls toward the M dwarf, but the frame is rotating, so the Coriolis force bends the stream to one side. At q = 0.27 my integration puts the impact about 15° ahead of the line joining the two bodies. The paper gets 14.8° and a phase offset of Δφ ≈ 0.04, which matches the observed peak at φ ≈ 0.44–0.48.

Why the colours differ so much. A small patch much hotter than a 2,700 K photosphere adds a lot of blue light and very little red. Fitting a single blackbody spot to the two quoted amplitudes gives about 5,200 K covering about 2.5% of the visible disc, a radius of roughly 0.02 R☉. The paper gives Rhot ∼ 10−2 R☉. Same order, from a fit with two parameters. The real spot has a temperature gradient, so treat the right-hand panel as a toy.

Why it's stable. Both bodies are dense, and the donor (the lighter one) is losing mass. When a convective donor loses mass its Roche lobe shrinks more slowly than the star does, as long as q stays below about 2/3. Transfer then throttles itself instead of running away. The authors' argument is that finding two such systems at all means the phase lasts a long time, ≳ 107–108 years and plausibly gigayears.

03bWhy it's in this explainer

One link is honest. One isn't.

The honest link is about stability. In the morphogen lattice and in this binary, a durable structure is a process held in balance, not a thing at rest. Stripes are made and degraded every hour. The stream is lost and replaced every orbit. Each one has a sharp threshold, a two-cell floor in one case and q ≈ 2/3 in the other, past which the balance fails.

J0440's own period, 0.0602 d, gives residues 0.945 / 0.442 / 0.556 (mod 2 / 3 / 7). They aren't near anything, and Fig. 07 shows why that wouldn't matter either way.

The dishonest link would be to put J0440 into the book's §59 framework. Its period is orbital. In the book's own terms that's an "eclipser-like," geometry-set period, the class the book expects to carry little 7-structure. More to the point, the residue test can't tell structure from range width (tab 02, L5). So this system has nothing to add to that statistic, and the statistic has nothing to say about it.
