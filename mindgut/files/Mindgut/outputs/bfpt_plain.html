A plain explainer

BFPT, plainly

What it is, what it does, and why it might matter — a five-minute standalone explainer.

1. The thing itself

BFPT is a way of relabeling any digital file so that every tiny piece of it carries two tags instead of one.

That's the whole invention. Everything else follows from it.

Take any file — a text message, a photo, a song. Underneath, it's a long string of 0s and 1s. BFPT chops that string into little three-bit pieces. There are only eight possible three-bit pieces, from 000 to 111, so you can number them 0 through 7.

Then for each piece, BFPT writes down two things:

  1. The address. Which of the eight pieces was it? (0, 1, 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, or 7.)
  2. The role. Which of four categories does that number belong to?

The four categories are just "the number mod 3" — sorted by what happens when you divide by three:

RoleContainsMeaning of the label
N null0the boundary marker
A assertion1, 4, 7numbers that leave remainder 1
R relation2, 5numbers that leave remainder 2
D dynamic3, 6numbers that divide evenly

So the number 5 becomes the token (R, 5). Role R, address 5. That's a BFPT token.

Why two tags and not one? Because the address alone lets you rebuild the file exactly (it's lossless). The role alone lets you see the file's shape without caring about the contents. Keeping both means you get reconstruction and structure in the same object.

2. What "shape" means, concretely

Run any file through BFPT and count how many tokens were tagged A, R, D, or N. You get four percentages that add up to 100%.

Here's the surprise: those percentages are weirdly stable across totally different files.

Run it on a Victorian novel, on a political pamphlet, on ancient philosophy, on a modern blog post — all of them come out with roughly the same distribution: around 33% A, 24% R, 27% D, 16% N. Fiction, non-fiction, ancient, contemporary. Same underlying shape.

That means the role-tags aren't measuring the meaning of the file. They're measuring something underneath meaning — some structural property of how bytes tend to be arranged when humans write things.

And because the shape is stable, deviations from it become interesting. A file that comes out heavily A-dominant is unusual. A file with almost no N is unusual. You now have a way to compare two files by their shape signature, not their content.

3. What "phase" adds

There's a second layer I've glossed over. Beyond the four roles, BFPT also tracks how tokens cluster into three phases, called 147, 258, and 369. These are just the same numbers grouped a different way — by what they leave when divided by 3, but this time treated as coordinates.

PhaseContainsFeels like
1471, 4, 7opening / asserting
2582, 5, 8connecting / relating
3693, 6, 9closing / releasing

Every piece of text lands somewhere on this three-way split. A given sentence, paragraph, or whole book has a phase profile: how much of it is opening, how much is connecting, how much is closing.

That's the second axis. Role tells you the local flavor of a token; phase tells you which of three "moods" the whole passage is drifting toward.

4. So what does it do?

Three practical things, in ascending order of interest:

It fingerprints text by shape.

Two documents that look different but have the same role-and-phase distribution are, in some real sense, structurally similar. Two documents by the same author often line up. Two documents on the same topic often don't. It's a measurement you couldn't otherwise take.

It makes drift visible.

If you run BFPT continuously on an unfolding conversation, you can watch the balance tip in real time. A conversation slowly sliding into pure abstraction, or pure agreement, or pure argument, shows up as the numbers leaning before anyone would name it in words.

It gives text a coordinate system.

Normally, if you want to find a passage in a long document, you search by keyword. With BFPT, every passage also carries a shape signature — so you can search by what other passage resembles this one structurally, even if the words share nothing in common. Similarity by shape becomes something you can navigate, not just intuit.

5. What it is not

It doesn't compress files. A BFPT token is still three bits per three bits of input.

It doesn't understand meaning. It knows nothing about words, sentences, or ideas. It only knows about bit patterns.

It doesn't make a language model smarter. It's a measurement instrument that runs alongside a model, not inside it.

It doesn't do anything magical. There is no hidden physics, no claim about consciousness. It's a codec plus a statistics readout.

6. Why it matters anyway

Because measurement instruments change what's thinkable. Before thermometers, "temperature" was a feeling. After thermometers, it was a number, and once it was a number you could plot it, compare it, predict it, and eventually build engines and refrigerators from what you learned.

BFPT is that kind of instrument for text. It takes something we previously only had vibes about — the shape of a piece of writing, the tilt of a conversation, the similarity between two documents at a structural level — and turns it into numbers you can watch.

Whether those numbers will turn out to be as useful as temperature is not yet known. The instrument works. What it will let people see is still being figured out.

The whole thing in one line: BFPT relabels every three-bit chunk of a file with a role and an address, and once you have those labels, you can see the shape of any text without reading a word of it.
