I · Preamble

The Wedge Opens From a Point

Where the third phase velocity comes from, why the wedge has a shape, and what happens at the critical angle when the amber marker slides through zero.

The Mechanism, in GeometryDe Lorenci & Pereira · PRA 99 · 2019

Most transparent crystals split light into two paths. Birefringence is a geometry of orientation. What we're looking at is a more fragile state.

A hyperbolic medium is one where the principal permittivity along the anisotropy axis (ε∥) has gone negative, while the perpendicular permittivity (ε⊥) stays positive. The surface of allowed wave vectors — the shape traced out by every direction light can travel — opens from a closed ellipsoid into an unbounded hyperboloid. The two-mode ceiling of ordinary optics no longer holds.

Every hyperboloid has an asymptotic cone. Wave vectors that lie exactly along that cone are the borderline case: they mark the angle where the linear discriminant vanishes. In a linear hyperbolic medium, that cone is a hard wall — angles outside the cone admit real phase velocities, angles inside do not. There is no third ray, only two, and the cone is where they run out.

The nonlinear term σ² is what admits propagation inside the cone. It adds a fixed positive amount to the discriminant, independent of angle, and inside the forbidden region that fixed amount is what keeps the square root real. Three phase velocities coexist. Where does σ come from? It's the coupling

σ = μ′ E B / (2 μ² ε∥)

— what you get when you apply crossed external electric and magnetic fields to a medium whose magnetic response itself depends on the electric field it's carrying. σ is a knob you turn from outside the crystal. Turn it to zero and the forbidden interior stays forbidden; the wedge shuts to a single line on the optic axis. Turn it up and the wedge opens — the interior of the cone becomes a corridor where a third ray can live.

At the critical angle — 27.65° for this material's numbers, the exact half-angle of the asymptotic cone — the third phase velocity passes through zero. That's a sharp transition in the math (a real solution crossing zero is a genuine phase boundary, not a plotting artifact), but nothing dramatic happens to the wave itself. It persists on both sides. What inverts is its relation to the wave vector.

The stadium-wave image is exact: the pattern of raised arms can sweep around the stadium clockwise while every individual person walks forward through the aisle. Phase velocity is the pattern; group velocity is the people. At the critical angle the pattern reverses direction; the people keep walking.

In the instrument below, the amber marker on the phase-velocity needle doesn't disappear at 27.65°. It slides across the origin and reappears on the opposite side. The marker's position varies smoothly the whole way through — nothing jumps in the trace. What jumps is what the position means: on one side of the boundary it reads as a forward-phase mode co-moving with the ordinary ray; on the other side it reads as a backward-phase mode whose energy still flows toward the screen. Continuity in the drawing, discontinuity in the interpretation, and the two are locked to each other by the geometry of the radical.

The detector strip below the two upper views is where that becomes literal. The upper panels track phase — the pattern in the stadium. The strip tracks group — the person walking through the aisle, computed from the local slope of the phase-velocity surface. When the amber marker on the needle crosses the origin and the phase reverses, watch the amber landing on the detector plane: it doesn't cross to the other side. The ray lands where the ray lands. Two views, same solution, one telling you what inverts and the other telling you what doesn't.

The object of the piece is that marker. Not the crystal, not the equations, not the wedge as a region on the plot — the amber dot's traversal across the critical angle, the moment where continuous motion and inverted meaning happen at the same point. Pull σ to zero to see the corridor shut. Pull it back up to see it open. Sweep θ across 27.65° to see the marker cross the origin and flip its sense without breaking its trace.

At the Critical Angle

θ = 27.65° — the exact half-angle of the asymptotic cone. The amber marker ve− sits on the origin. It has not disappeared; it is passing through zero.

The ice ring is the ordinary ray — its phase speed vo = 1/√(με⊥) ≈ 0.820, independent of direction. The two extraordinary branches (ve+ in laminar, ve− in amber) come from the roots

ve± = −σ q̂z ± √( σ² q̂z² + q̂x²/(με⊥) + q̂z²/(με∥) )

with the sign on ε∥ kept negative throughout, so the third term inside the radical is negative and the sum survives only where σ² lifts it back above zero. Set σ = 0 and only angles outside the cone (θ > 27.65°) admit real roots — the ordinary birefringent wedge. Set σ ≠ 0 and the corridor inside the cone opens.

III · The Object

What the Amber Marker Is Doing

Three configurations to read side by side. In every one, the same ve− solution is there. What changes is where it lives.

Reading the Three Statesσ · θ · what the marker means

σ = 0. The corridor is shut. The amber curve on the right-hand plot exists only for θ > 27.65°, and even there it sits at the same radius as the laminar curve — the two extraordinary rays are mirror partners of a single split, not two distinct solutions. On the left, no amber marker inside the cone. This is the linear regime: two rays, one wedge boundary, no third root.

σ = −0.4894 (paper). The corridor is open. Amber and laminar curves separate; a third real phase speed exists at every angle. On the left, three markers ride the needle. The amber marker inside the cone (θ < 27.65°) is co-moving with the ordinary ray — its phase velocity points along the wave vector. Turn the θ slider up.

θ = 27.65° (at boundary). The amber marker is at the origin. The needle has three markers, but two of them are at nonzero radii and the third is at zero. On the right, the amber curve crosses the horizontal axis. Push θ another 0.05° higher.

θ > 27.65°. The amber marker has re-emerged — but on the opposite side of the origin, on the back half of the needle. Its trace is continuous; the arrow from origin to marker has reversed. The wave has not disappeared, has not slowed, has not scattered. Its phase velocity vector now points against the wave vector. The group velocity — the direction energy actually flows, the direction a ray drawn on a screen would go — remains forward. This is a backward-phase wave, the same object the stadium wave describes: the pattern reverses; the people keep walking.

The Larger Rhymecontinuity through inversion
A continuous parameter inverts directional meaning without breaking form. That is not a feature of one crystal. It is a class of behaviour — the class into which the monopole, the phase-cone, and the third root all rhyme.
