How a moth works

Drop the idea of a drab nighttime butterfly. Moths are roughly nine in ten of all Lepidoptera, and butterflies are one moth lineage that took the day shift about 100 million years ago.

The rest stayed in the dark, where photons are scarce, the air is cold, scent tears apart in turbulence and bats hunt by sonar. Night made them some of the most extreme sensory and mechanical systems on Earth.

Chapter 1● memory

Two animals sharing one genome

A moth has two jobs that pull against each other: eat as much as possible, and travel far through thin air to find a mate. It solves both by being two different machines in sequence.

Inside the pupa, enzymes break much of the caterpillar down. What carries on are the imaginal discs, packed clusters of embryonic cells that rode inside the larva since before it hatched, waiting for the hormonal signal to unfold into wings, eyes, legs and antennae.

The brain is rebuilt for flight, but not wiped. Caterpillars trained to avoid an odor still avoided it as adults.

Larva
A walking gut in a pressurized bag. No wings, almost no long-range vision, and a few thousand muscles for gripping and chewing.
Imago
A light, rigid chitin airframe driven by a few massive flight muscles, carrying chemical, visual and acoustic sensors.
Kept
Parts of the mushroom bodies, the insect brain's memory regions, pass through metamorphosis intact.

Source. Blackiston, Silva Casey & Weiss, 2008, in Manduca sexta. Memory carried over when training happened late in larval life. "Soup" is shorthand: the nervous system, tracheae and parts of the gut are remodeled, not dissolved.

Chapter 2● heat

The warm-blooded flight engine

At midnight in 8 °C air, insect flight muscle is too slow to make the 25 to 70 wingbeats a second that flight needs. Many moths solve it by running hot while active.

Before takeoff a hawkmoth fires its upstroke and downstroke muscles against each other. The wings barely move. The fuel goes to heat until the thorax reaches 35 to 40 °C, about human body temperature.

Fur
Dense thoracic scales insulate like mammal fur and keep heat in the flight motor.
Radiator
If hovering overheats it, the tubular heart sends hot hemolymph through the narrow waist into the bare abdomen, which sheds it to the night air.
Box
The big flight muscles pull on the thorax wall, not the wings. The shell rings at its resonant frequency and the wings are levered by it.
Vortex
Each stroke spins a leading-edge vortex that stays attached above the wing: low pressure, high lift, enough to hover.
Chapter 3● scent

Single-molecule chemical radar

The feathered antennae of a male silk moth are a sieve for molecules. Each branch carries thousands of hairs, each hair is pierced by pores, and each pore leads to neurons tuned to the female's pheromone blend. One molecule of bombykol on a receptor is enough to fire a nerve impulse.

Outdoors a scent is not a smooth gradient. Turbulence shreds the plume into ragged ribbons separated by clean air, so playing warmer and colder loses the trail at once. Moths run a two-state rule instead.

Surge
Odor on. The instant an antenna clips a filament, turn and fly straight upwind, reading wind from the ground sliding under the eyes.
Cast
Odor off. Stop advancing and zigzag across the wind in widening sweeps until the next ribbon is cut.

Scale. Around 100 to 200 molecules a second on the antenna is enough to launch a male, by Kaissling's measurements in Bombyx. Field tracking over kilometers is reported but hard to measure. Robots hunting gas leaks and hydrothermal vents use the same rule.

Chapter 4● ultrasound

Ears, stealth and jamming

For roughly 60 million years moths have been hunted by bats calling at 20 to over 100 kHz. Different families answered in different ways.

The two-neuron ear. A noctuid's eardrum feeds just two cells. A1 is sensitive and fires at faint ultrasound: a bat is about, not yet locked on. Comparing left and right tells the moth where, and it banks away. A2 needs about ten times the sound pressure. When both fire the bat is close, and steering gives way to loops, rolls and power dives.

Stealth
Earless silk moths wear an absorber. Wing scales each resonate at a slightly different pitch and together soak up as much as 72% of a sonar pulse.
Decoy
A luna's twisted tails spin and throw echoes behind the body. Bats aimed at the tails in 55% of attacks and rarely caught the moth when they did.
Jam
Tiger moths click back from a ribbed tymbal at up to 4,500 clicks a second. Some are advertising toxins. Bertholdia trigona lands its clicks inside the bat's echo window and spoils the range estimate.

Caveat. The A1/A2 split is Kenneth Roeder's reading from the 1960s. Later recordings, including from moths whose ears have a single cell, question whether A2 alone triggers the dive. The rings in Fig. 4 are schematic, not to scale.

Chapter 5● light

Starlight color, and the porch light

In dim moonlight our cones starve and we see in grey. A day-flying butterfly's apposition eye is no better at night: each lens sits on a walled tube and feeds only its own sensor.

A nocturnal moth's superposition eye has a wide clear zone inside. Light entering hundreds of neighboring lenses is bent onto a single photoreceptor, which makes the eye up to about 1,000 times more sensitive. Elephant hawkmoths pick out blue, yellow and ultraviolet flowers by starlight.

That leaves the lamp. The old story was a moth steering by the moon and mistaking a bulb for it. But moths do not spiral in. They orbit, stall, flip over the bulb and fall.

They are not flying to the light. They are keeping their backs to it. In the dark, gravity is hard to feel through gusts and turns, and for hundreds of millions of years one cue held: the sky is brighter than the ground. Tilt your back toward the bright half and you stay level. A point of light beside or below you breaks the rule.

Source. Fabian, Sondhi and colleagues, 2024, filmed with infrared motion capture. The reflex explains why insects are trapped near a lamp. Whether it also draws them in from far away is still open. Starlight color: Kelber, Balkenius & Warrant, 2002.

Chapter 6● one species, close up

Io

Automeris io · a giant silk moth of eastern North America · described by Fabricius in 1775 and named for Io, a mortal loved by Zeus

By day it is a yellow leaf. The eyes are underneath.

Two liveries

Males are bright yellow, with broad feathered antennae for reading scent. Females are reddish-brown to purple, usually larger, with thin antennae.

The display

At rest the forewings cover the hindwings. Touched, the moth flexes them forward and shakes, and each hindwing shows one black-and-blue eye with a white center.

The audience

Birds, bats, small mammals and spiders are among its recorded predators. The display is a bluff: the adult has no sting and no bite.

Twenty to thirty-five eggs in a cluster. Ten days. Then five skins in about four weeks.

The young walk nose to tail, up to forty in a line. Lead the line onto its own tail and it circles.

The adult gets one to two weeks and no mouth. Nearly everything happens after dark.

Where

The eastern half of the United States, west to Utah, Colorado and Texas, north into southern Canada and south into Mexico and Costa Rica. One generation a year in the north, two to four in Florida and Texas, year-round in the Keys.

Trouble

Numbers in the eastern United States have fallen and become more local. An introduced parasitoid fly, Compsilura concinnata, is among its recorded enemies. How much of the decline it explains is not settled by the sources used here.

Where the sources differ

Wingspan is given as 63–88 mm in one and 50–90 mm in another. The sting is called a venom in one, while the other notes that the spine contents have not been analyzed in this species.

A moth at a streetlamp is not mesmerized.

Its horizon reflex has been handed a point of light that never existed while the reflex evolved.
