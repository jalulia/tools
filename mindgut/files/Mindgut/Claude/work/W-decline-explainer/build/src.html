01
The signal

A wrist knows things a clinic never sees.

A clinic visit is twenty minutes, twice a year, with the lights on and the patient trying. A watch is twenty-four hours a day with nobody trying. That is the whole premise: not a better test, but more of life in the sample.

This strip is simulated, not a real recording. It shows the three things the Parkinson's work actually measured: how much the arm swings when you walk, how much of the waking day has a 4–6 Hz tremor in it, and how much you move at all. Drag the year slider to move a hypothetical person along the twelve-month change WATCH-PD reported.

Real numbers underneath. WATCH-PD, 82 people with early untreated Parkinson's: mean arm swing fell from 25.9° to 19.9° in a year; waking time with tremor rose from 19.3% to 25.6%. Those are group means, not one person's year.

02
The headline

"Seven years before diagnosis" is true. It is also the least useful sentence in the paper.

The 2023 Nature Medicine study looked backwards. UK Biobank had wrist data on 100,000 people; years later, 113 of them were diagnosed with Parkinson's. Their old wrist data, re-read, was already different from everyone else's, up to seven years before anyone said the word.

So the signal exists. The question is what it is worth at the moment you'd want to use it, which is before you know who the 113 are. Drag the line. Left of it is "flag this person"; right of it is "leave them alone."

What the study reported: for prodromal cases, area under the precision–recall curve 0.07 ± 0.03, against roughly 0.01 for genetics, 0.03 for lifestyle, 0.01 for blood. Several times better than anything else. Still far from a test. The curves here are illustrative shapes consistent with that result, not the paper's data.

03
The ladder

Four different claims hide inside the word "detect."

Almost every confusion in this field is one of these rungs being mistaken for the one above it. A study can stand firmly on rung one and get reported as rung four. Tap a study to see how high it actually climbs.

Rung 1

It measures

The number from the device agrees with a trusted instrument or a clinician's score. Arm swing on the watch matches arm swing in the gait lab.

Rung 2

It separates

People who already have the disease score differently from people who don't. Useful. Also the easiest thing to show, and the easiest to overclaim.

Rung 3

It predicts

Today's signal says something about who gets worse, or who gets diagnosed, later. This needs years and patience. Few studies have both.

Rung 4

It helps

Acting on the signal makes someone's life go better than not acting. Nothing in the review reaches this rung for a consumer device. Nothing.

04
The anchor

Blue is what makes red mean something.

A remote memory test on a phone gives you a score. On its own that score is just a score: tired, distracted, bad day, bad phone. The Alzheimer's work got interesting when the same people also had amyloid and tau PET scans. Now each red dot has a blue partner, and the question becomes: does the cheap signal track the expensive truth?

In the Mayo Test Drive cohort (684 adults, 94% cognitively unimpaired) it did: lower remote scores went with more amyloid, more tau, smaller hippocampi. Not perfectly. Drag the slider to see what a weak, medium or strong relationship looks like with the same number of people, and watch what happens to the confidence you can have about one person.

Then one step further. BioCog (2025) was the first to put the digital test next to a blood test in an independent primary-care cohort of 403: digital alone reached 85% for cognitive impairment against doctors' 73%; digital plus blood reached 90% for biomarker-confirmed Alzheimer's. Still a clinical population, not the general public. But it is the closest thing in the review to a pathway you could actually run.

05
The leak

How a model gets 96% on the test and fails the first stranger.

Accelerometer data is sliced into windows, a few seconds each. One person wearing a watch for a week produces tens of thousands of windows, and all of them carry that person's fingerprint: their height, their shoes, their particular wobble.

If you shuffle the windows and split them randomly into "train" and "test," the model sees the same people on both sides. It learns to recognise people, not disease, and the test score soars. Split by person instead, and the score falls to what it really is. Toggle the split.

This is the single most common error in the deep-learning papers. The review's validation rule one: separate by participant before windowing. The ElderNet result worth remembering here: 96.43% "accuracy" was for detecting walking, with recall 82%, not for diagnosing anything.

06
The dropout

The watch stops. Was that the disease, or the charger?

Everything above assumes the data arrives. In the one-year Parkinson's smartwatch study, 200 people started and 116 finished. Of the 118 reasons given for stopping, 61 were technical: battery, syncing, the app. People who were doing worse also stopped more. So the dataset you end up analysing is quietly the dataset of people who were doing fine and had a working charger.

Each line is a person's wear time over the year. Drag the slider to see how much of your cohort you lose, and then look at who is left.

Floodlight Open, the MS app, measured this directly: simplifying the activation screen took successful activation from 53.9% to 74.6%. The onboarding flow changed the science. That is why "usability" sits in the review next to "validation," not after it.

The plumbing

Qualtrics asks. REDCap remembers. Neither one is "HIPAA compliant" by itself.

Millicent's question had a specific study shape in it: daily cognitive surveys go out through Qualtrics, the patient registry lives in REDCap. That is a reasonable design. It is not a standard one, and the review could not find a neurology study that validates it as such. So here it is as a diagram of what each piece should and should not hold.

Hover or tap a box. The rule underneath all of it: the thing that knows your name and the thing that knows your score should be two different systems, joined by a token that means nothing on its own.

Two traps. Putting a validated paper memory test into a web survey does not make the web version validated: browser timing, screen size and a tremor all change the task. And REDCap's own wording is that it "can be installed in environments supporting compliance," which is a statement about your IT department, not about the software.

08
So

Not a watch that diagnoses. A service that notices, then asks.

Put the instruments together and the shape of what is actually buildable falls out. Not a single score that says "Parkinson's." A staircase: a quiet baseline learned over months; a flag only when the change persists (one bad week is a bad week); a short active task to confirm it; a clinician looking at it with the person's history; and only then the expensive blue test, PET or CSF or blood, for the people it would change something for.

Each step cuts the false alarms from the last one. That is what the threshold instrument was really about: you never get to move the line far enough on one signal alone, so you stack signals and let each one move it a little.

What's ready now: prescreening for trials; monitoring people already diagnosed; the BioCog-style digital-plus-blood triage. What isn't: screening the general public with a consumer device, any claim that acting on the signal early improves lives. Nobody has run that trial yet.
