## 1. The desk, as I understand the build

Two textual lenses on a shared relation set; the reader chooses and combines them; the diagram (engraved linework) and the reading queue are both renders of the same relation data; the working question is a text field the reader alters as the pair changes. Real titles; authored analytical notes, labelled as such; no fake quotation, no Mir recall as subject; source inspection available but secondary. Shared-state architecture from `crx01` (one declaration, many renders), x-height annotation sizing from `d1`, integer-snapped tracks from `e2`. White, Archivo normal width, mono for addresses, violet/teal on marks only.

The one thing I'd hold the build to: **a pairing must produce a claim-level difference the diagram can draw.** "These two books are both about memory" is a similarity and draws nothing. "A says X is a property of the artifact; B says X is a property of the reader" is a difference and draws as two edges from one node to two different nodes. Every pairing below is chosen so the difference is of that kind.

## 2. Three pairings with precise differences

All are well-established works. The notes are my analysis, written for the desk, and should be labelled "authored note" on the page. Codex can verify every factual claim below against the texts.

### Pairing A — *The Death and Life of Great American Cities* (Jane Jacobs, 1961) × *Seeing Like a State* (James C. Scott, 1998)

**Shared node:** legibility of a city.
**The precise difference:** Jacobs argues that street-level *diversity* — mixed primary uses, short blocks, buildings of varied age, density — produces order that is **real but illegible to planners** (the "sidewalk ballet"); her remedy is to *protect* it from planning. Scott argues that states make societies legible in order to govern them (cadastral maps, surnames, grid cities, scientific forestry) and that the **high-modernist** project fails because the legible model discards the local practical knowledge (*mētis*) it cannot see. Scott cites Jacobs at length in chapter 4 and treats her as an ally.
**So where do they actually differ?** On *agency*. Jacobs locates the order in the people of the street and the remedy in civic resistance to planning; Scott locates the failure in the state's **epistemic** method and treats mētis as a general category that includes but exceeds Jacobs' streets. In relation data: `Jacobs: order ← street diversity (the planner cannot see it)`; `Scott: failure ← legibility-seeking (the state cannot see mētis)`. Same shared node, opposite direction of the edge: Jacobs' edge points *at the thing that works*; Scott's points *at the method that breaks it.*
**Working question it generates:** *When a system becomes easier to read, what has it stopped being able to see?*
**Why it suits Julia's work:** her record holds spatial reasoning (POP boards, a café bay sized at real cover dimensions, 36 facings), and a research dossier on legibility of her own archive. This pairing is about exactly that.

### Pairing B — *The Design of Everyday Things* (Donald Norman, 1988; revised 2013) × *The Structure of Scientific Revolutions* (Thomas Kuhn, 1962)

**Shared node:** error.
**The precise difference:** Norman's central claim is that most "human error" is **design error** — the artifact lacked affordances, signifiers, a good conceptual model, feedback — and the remedy is to redesign the object so the error is impossible or recoverable. Kuhn's claim is that *anomaly* — observation that fails the paradigm's expectations — is not a design fault to be removed but the **engine of change**: normal science absorbs anomalies until it cannot, and then the paradigm shifts. For Norman, error is information about the object; for Kuhn, anomaly is information about the *framework*. In relation data: `Norman: error → redesign the artifact`; `Kuhn: anomaly → (resist, then) replace the paradigm`.
**Working question it generates:** *Is this failure telling me about the thing, or about the frame I'm using to look at it?*
**Why it suits Julia's work:** her documentation corrections (760, 764, 884) are Norman-type — the artifact was wrong, fix the artifact. But the "disembodied people" correction (974) was Kuhn-type: the reference was *read* wrong; the frame changed, not the render. The desk lets a reader sort their own corrections that way without the desk being about Mir.

### Pairing C — *Gödel, Escher, Bach* (Douglas Hofstadter, 1979) × *Musicophilia* (Oliver Sacks, 2007)

**Shared node:** what music does to a mind.
**The precise difference:** Hofstadter uses Bach's canons and fugues as *formal* objects — self-reference, recursion, "strange loops" — and argues that mind (and meaning) arise from such self-referential formal structure; the music is evidence for a theory of symbols. Sacks reports *clinical* cases — musical hallucinations, amusia, musical epilepsy, music reaching patients with dementia or Parkinsonism — and argues that music's hold on the brain is a **neurological** fact prior to any formal account; the music is evidence about bodies. In relation data: `Hofstadter: meaning ← formal self-reference (music as instance)`; `Sacks: response ← neural organization (music as cause)`. Same node; one edge goes to *structure*, the other to *physiology*.
**Working question it generates:** *What in this piece is doing the work — its form, or what it does to a listener?*
**Why it suits Julia's work:** the record holds sound work (the Sandman chord instrument; the walkthrough scores; the Spotify export she mentioned to the team). This is the pairing for the sound family. **Caution:** Sacks is a clinician writing about patients; the desk's notes should stay at the level of his *argument* and not reproduce case material. Nothing medical enters the page.

## 3. How changing the pair changes the working question — one worked example

Start with Pairing A. The reader's working question, typed into the field: *When a system becomes easier to read, what has it stopped being able to see?* The diagram shows the shared node `legibility`, Jacobs' edge to `street diversity`, Scott's edge to `legibility-seeking method`. The queue lists Jacobs ch. 2–4 (the uses of sidewalks) and Scott ch. 1 and 4 beside each other.

Now the reader **swaps Scott for Norman** (keeping Jacobs). The shared node changes — `legibility` no longer connects; the strongest shared node between Jacobs and Norman is **feedback**: Jacobs' "eyes on the street" is a feedback system (the street watches itself); Norman's feedback is the artifact reporting the result of an action. The diagram redraws from the same relation data: the edge from Jacobs now lands on `feedback`, and Norman's edge lands there from the other side. The queue reorders: Jacobs ch. 2 stays, Scott drops out, Norman ch. 1 and 2 (affordances, signifiers, feedback) come in.

The working question *should* change and the field says so — the text the reader wrote is now **marked** (an x-height caret from `d1`, violet) as *written under a previous pair*. It isn't rewritten for them. The reader edits it: *What does the system tell its users about what they just did — and who is listening?* The old question stays legible in the field's history, struck, with the pair it belonged to.

That is the whole interaction: **pair → shared node → edges → queue**, all from one relation object; the question is the only thing the reader owns, and the desk never writes it for them — it only tells them when the ground under it moved.

## 4. Short critique of the direction

- **Risk 1 — the pairings are the content, and content ages.** Three authored pairings are enough for a prototype; if the desk is ever more than a study, the relation data has to be editable by the reader, or it becomes a curated essay with controls. Fine for LB-01; say so in the colophon.
- **Risk 2 — "shared node" can be forced.** Any two books share *something*. The build should refuse to draw a pairing whose shared node has fewer than two authored notes behind it — the diagram going blank is the honest state, and it's the same discipline as V01's "no bracket, no change."
- **Risk 3 — it can drift back to a brand page.** Engraved linework is beautiful; if the diagram gets more drawing than the relation data justifies, it's ornament again. Rule from `crx01`: nothing on the diagram that isn't a field in the relation object.
- **Risk 4 — Mir's absence has to be deliberate, not accidental.** The desk has no Mir in it, which is right for the subject. But the hub is the Mir Study. One line in the colophon should say why: *source inspection is the Mindgut idea on this page; the desk shows it applied to someone else's sources.* Otherwise the reader asks what this has to do with Mir and the answer isn't on the page.
- **Where it's strong:** it is the first thing in the hub where the reader *thinks* rather than watches a correction happen. That answers the criticism directly.

## 5. Statement — revised for Gaby's request

Gaby's brief (strand 994): *what it is, how it works, why it's exceptional; travel-safe, no proprietary reveal, written so someone at a lab could read it and say "that's an interesting claim."* Julia's perspective; only uses her record holds; no promise of flawless identity or recall; no catchphrase; no competitor universal.

> Mindgut is a way of working with an AI model in which the record of the work is kept apart from the model. Every exchange is saved with who said it — me or the model. The model is given the recent conversation directly; earlier exchanges are brought back when they bear on what is in front of us, and I can see which ones were used. The record stays with me, and it has carried across a change of model.
>
> I have used it on different kinds of work: research into an archive of my own projects, sizing a small shop from real dimensions, documentation, interactive prototypes, and sound. What holds these together is not a feature. It is that a judgment made in one of them is still there, with its reason and its scope, when the next one starts.
>
> The claim I think is worth a stranger's attention is this: an AI's memory can be a record a person holds and can audit, in which what the model proposed and what I decided remain distinguishable, and a recollection with no human source behind it is marked as such rather than offered as fact. It is not infallible. Mir has asserted things the record did not support, and the record is how that was caught.
>
> The test is not whether an AI remembers, but whether what it remembers can be checked.
