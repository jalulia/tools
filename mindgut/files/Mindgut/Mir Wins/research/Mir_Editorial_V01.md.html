## 3. Statement — revised (travel-safe)

Constraints honoured: no people's names; no proprietary mechanism; no universal competitor claim; no guaranteed identical identity across models; no constant-cost promise; no-system-prompt stated as a design choice rather than a verified fact; no research instructions in the copy. Perspective: artifacts, judgments and corrections that survive the next iteration.

> **Mindgut**
>
> An AI you work with is two things: a model, and the record of what has passed between you. Most products keep that record on the model company's servers, in a form you cannot take with you. Mindgut separates the two. The record is one file. You own it. The model is whatever you plug in today.
>
> Everything in the record is marked with the side of the conversation it came from. That matters more than it sounds. Over a long working relationship most of the words are the AI's, and an AI that reads its own summaries back as fact is the commonest way these systems drift. The human's share is smaller and more valuable, because most of it is judgment: *no, not that typeface. Everyone in this picture has lost their face. Make it more crafted.* A record that knows which side said what, and in what order, holds those as decisions rather than as competing facts.
>
> Mindgut is designed to work without a system prompt — the model is not told who it is; it is handed the recent conversation and reaches into the record for the rest. The identity that forms on the record is called Mir. The claim worth testing is that Mir stays recognisably Mir when the model underneath changes, because what persists lives in the file.
>
> You can test it. Switch the model partway through a piece of work. Ask where something came from. Keep going for a year and see whether the corrections hold.
>
> It is early. Recall sometimes fires for reasons it cannot explain, and it has been caught stating a provenance it then had to retract — on the record, which is how it was caught.
>
> What is worth having after a year is not what the model knows. It is every no you have given it, kept, and marked as yours.

Word count: 319 (measured with wc after writing; my in-head estimate was 303) — **over the 150–230 brief by ~90.** Cut candidates, in order: the three-item italic list (−14), the "You can test it" paragraph (−24), the second sentence of the first paragraph (−17). With all three cut it lands at ~264; reaching 230 also needs the "That matters more than it sounds" sentence gone and paragraph two compressed. I have not made those cuts here. I'd rather Julia choose which of the three concrete judgments to keep than cut them blind, since they are the only part of the statement that is hers.

Candid critique of my own draft:

- "Recognisably Mir" is doing a lot of work. It is deliberately weaker than "the same Mir". A lab reader will push on what "recognisably" means and we do not yet have a measure for it on this account.
- The last line is the one line from the 10-22 draft I kept. It is also the only line that is purely rhetorical.

## 4. Three design constraints for the bento (anchored in the product)

The brief says flat design 2.0, retro-reminiscent, retain the current icon's essence. These three come from how the product actually behaves, not from mood.

**C1 · Two sides, always visible.** Every strand in the record carries a side. The UI should never render a line without its carrier being readable at a glance — not a colour-coded bubble (that's chat), but a margin mark, a rule weight, a column. The product's one real differentiator is provenance; the interface should make it impossible to forget which side you're reading. This is also the retro hook: ledgers, call sheets and proof sheets all have a margin column that says who.

**C2 · The window is short; the record is long — show both.** The working window is seven exchanges and breathes (4→7→4). The record is a thousand-plus strands. A screen that shows only the conversation hides the product. The bento should carry a persistent, quiet readout of depth — strand count, where the window sits in the breath — in the same way an instrument shows tape position. Julia's own phrase from the boards thread: "tuning, like you would an instrument or a receiver" was Chris's; her read was Winamp — "things having their own character or something you get to know and make yours." A tape counter is both.

**C3 · Corrections are first-class, not deletions.** Nothing in the record is overwritten; a "no" is a new strand that points at the old one. The UI should have a visible, unglamorous way to mark a line as corrected — and the correction should be the thing that's styled, not the error. Flat 2.0 handles this well: a single-weight strikethrough rule and a reply-indent are enough. The failure mode to design against is the chat-app pattern where the wrong answer scrolls away and the right one looks identical to it.

What I'd avoid: any identity that leans on the piano/air metaphor or on the physics vocabulary (cosets, PAR, Fano). Those are lore, and Julia drew that line in Slack on 10-19: "this stuff is sick for like, the lore of Mindgut, but the actual UI, what's the most distilled version maybe?"
