I

What the Jargon Was Reaching For

"Yingangaxial trirefringence" is two real things wearing one invented coat. Take the coat off and both things underneath are better than the coat.

Ground Truthwhat exists · what doesn't

Trirefringence is real. The "yin/yang axis" framing was mine. Here is exactly where the seam is.

Real: ordinary transparent crystals split light into at most two rays — birefringence, calcite's double image, known since 1669. In 2012, De Lorenci & Pereira showed that a class of nonlinear metamaterials can support three rays from one wave direction — genuine trirefringence, published in Phys. Rev. A 86, 013801, revisited and generalized in 2019.

Real: biaxial crystals — aragonite, mica — have three principal refractive indices (nα < nβ < nγ). Not three rays: three axes of the index ellipsoid. And along two special directions the two-ray solution degenerates into something stranger than any finite count of rays: a hollow cone of light. Hamilton predicted it from pure mathematics in October 1832; Humphrey Lloyd found it in aragonite within two months. It was the first time in history a mathematical theory predicted a qualitatively new phenomenon before anyone had seen it.

Invented: the word "yingangaxial," and the toy I built earlier — three independent Snell's-law rays with hand-tuned indices. Charming, but the physics was decorative. The intuition it was reaching for, though, was pointed at something true: in a three-index medium, the middle index is where the strangeness lives.

Two Sheets

Two curves, two speeds, two rays — ordinary birefringence. Drag θ toward the marked angle and watch the sheets approach each other.

The plot is the wave surface section in the nα–nγ plane: for each direction, the two curves mark the two phase-speed solutions. In a biaxial crystal the sheets touch at four conoidal points — the optic axes. At exactly that direction there aren't two answers. The equation has a degenerate double root, and the light resolves it by becoming a cone.

Move the nβ slider. As the middle index approaches either pole (nα or nγ), the optic axes migrate toward the principal axes and the crystal degenerates toward uniaxial — the third thing collapses into one of the two poles, and the cone vanishes. The strangeness exists only while the middle index is strictly between the poles. That is the true content the yin/yang framing was groping at, and the real version is sharper than the invented one.

III

Three Rays, For Real

Where genuine trirefringence actually lives — and why it needs nonlinearity to exist.

De Lorenci & Pereira · 2012 / 2019Phys. Rev. A 86 013801 · 99 053841
RegimeRaysWhat it takes
Isotropic1Glass, water. One index, one ray. Nothing to resolve.
Birefringent2Any anisotropic linear crystal. The quartic wave equation factors into two quadratics — at most two phase speeds per direction. This is a hard ceiling for linear media.
Trirefringent3A nonlinear uniaxial metamaterial with one negative permittivity component and field-dependent permeability. The nonlinearity breaks the factoring; three phase-speed solutions with linearly independent polarizations coexist in one direction. One incident ray leaves as three.

The structural point worth keeping: two is the ceiling for linear relation. A linear medium, however anisotropic, can only ever answer a direction with two speeds — the math factors, pole against pole. To get a third simultaneous answer, the medium itself has to respond to the light that is asking — permeability that depends on the field passing through it. The third ray is not another pole. It is what becomes possible when the relation stops being one-way.
