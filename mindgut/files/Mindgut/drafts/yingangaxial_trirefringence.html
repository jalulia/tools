Yingangaxial Trirefringence

A hypothetical optical medium with three principal refractive axes — yin, yang, and the axial third that keeps them from collapsing into each other. One ray enters; three emerge, each polarized along its own axis. Drag the sliders. Watch what the third axis does.

Ordinary crystals are at most birefringent — two axes, two rays, calcite's famous double image. Trirefringence is the invented third case: a medium whose symmetry is so low that yin (slow, dense, accepting) and yang (fast, sparse, asserting) cannot account for the light between them, and a third axial ray carries the remainder.

Snell's law applies per axis: sin θ₁ = n sin θ₂ — a larger index bends the ray harder toward the normal. The axial ray sits between the poles; when you lock it to the geometric mean √(nyin·nyang), it becomes the exact refractive midpoint — the relation that holds the two poles apart. Unlock it and it is free to be its own thing.

Past ±41° the steepest axis hits total internal reflection first and its ray dies at the boundary — the poles fail one at a time, the relation last.
